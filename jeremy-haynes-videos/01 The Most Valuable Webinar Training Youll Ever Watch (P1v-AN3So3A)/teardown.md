# Teardown: The Most Valuable Webinar Training You'll Ever Watch

Jeremy Haynes · uploaded 2026-10-03 · 1:40:45 · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P1v-AN3So3A
Files here: `transcript.md` (timestamps), `study.md` (frames + words), `sheets/` (frames). Torn down 2026-10-29
(`sops/video-teardown.md`). Stamps are `[m:ss]` from the captions; frame stamps can sit up to about 10 s off.

## In one paragraph

A complete playbook for a live webinar sold to cold paid traffic, where the webinar books sales calls and the calls
close a high-ticket offer. It runs in twelve numbered parts, from the test budget to scaling. The spine is a
financial model with a benchmark for every step of the funnel [1:59], messaging built on one "majority hook" and a
unique mechanism written as a single headline that every asset then satisfies [12:46, 22:47], his "back-end selling
systems" that warm people up between opt-in and the live webinar [30:45], a webinar of about an hour of teaching
plus an hour or more of closing [1:13:55], and a bottleneck analysis that tells you which single number to fix
[1:29:55]. It is taught live on an iPad legal pad, drawn by hand on a big screen behind him (see "What is on
screen"), and it sells his Jeremy AI tool (a free webinar-slide builder) and his Jeremy's Inner Circle program
throughout.

## The frameworks

**The casino test** [2:09]. The test budget is what you can lose and still come back tomorrow: "walk into the
casino, be able to vaporize the money, and still have willingness to walk back into the casino the next day."
Budget per webinar times four is the monthly exposure at one webinar a week [2:19].

**Cash, not revenue** [5:18]. Model on cash collected: "Optimize around the actual dollars that hit your payment
processor."

**Optimize for cold** [7:37]. Even an organic (warm) webinar is built for cold viewers, because a cold-optimized
webinar still converts warm, and a warm-optimized one fails cold.

**Cliff and bridge** [8:22]. One cliff is where the buyer is (problems and circumstances), the other is where they
want to be (outcomes); the offer is the bridge. "Only then when they feel understood for where they are and where
they want to go, do they care about the bridge" [8:34].

**The dart system** [9:16]. Feed algorithms (organic or paid) guess until they hit what a person wants, then fill
the feed with that thing and everything near it (the car example: cars, then convertibles, trucks, motorcycles,
scooters, a kids' car [~10:30]). So a lead goes from no awareness to hyper aware and hyper pitched within a few
scrolls, which is why messaging has to assume they have seen every competitor.

**Majority hook vs minority hook** [12:46]. Speak to the problem, circumstance or outcome the largest share of
financially qualified buyers shares ("contextual relatability to the broadest demographic of financially qualified
buyers" [12:33]), never a small slice of it. Story: Spencer's software was sold on "get your show rate up"
(minority); the majority hook was the new idea he said in passing, "sales ops", the part between lead generation
and the sales call [13:04-15:24].

**Market awareness and sophistication** [16:33]. From Eugene Schwartz's Breakthrough Advertising: talk to people as
aware and as pitched as they now are. Combined with the dart system, every lead is more aware and more pitched than
ever.

**Unique mechanism** [19:04]. In a saturated feed, you must have your own way of getting the outcome, and it is the
main thing worked into the messaging.

**The theme, and "think in headlines"** [20:25, 22:14]. Before any asset, write the webinar's theme as one headline
that names the majority outcomes. His: "The back-end blueprint: how smart business owners are getting higher show
rates, more closes, and shorter sales cycles" [20:25]. Then every ad, email, text, page, the presentation and the
offer satisfy that headline's claim. He built this video the same way: "if somebody just watched one video on
webinars ... the last webinar training they'd ever need, how would I satisfy that?" [22:31].

**The back-end selling systems** [30:45-39:39]. His five strategies for the time between opt-in and the sale,
because "they're not sold yet" [34:22]:
1. the confirmation page (below);
2. "hammer them": 12 to 20 pieces of short and long video content put in front of the lead in the feed where they
   were found, before the webinar [38:46];
3. setters who reach out before the webinar or call to give value about what the person opted in for, not to
   qualify [~39:05];
4. value-dense email sequences [39:21];
5. "AI manipulation mastery": getting AI models to say positive things about you [39:37].

**The confirmation page** [30:45-42:35]. The headline acknowledges the action ("You're in. Here's what to do next."
[32:16]). The first video is one of two kinds [32:56]: an **urgency video** (why act now; the co-living example) or
a **research / due diligence video** (radical transparency to build trust fast: his dispute rate, refunds, top and
average results, true averages [34:31]). Then **breakout videos**: one per question that, left unanswered, makes
people show up colder; take them from what the sales team is tired of explaining and from the "aha moments" of
the best buyers [40:42]. Then testimonials. Order: urgency or due diligence video, breakout videos, testimonials
[42:04].

**Scanner mode vs justification mode** [42:31]. "On the front end, they're in scanner mode. On the back end,
they're in justification mode": after opting in, people are deciding whether to show up at all.

**Emails teach, texts remind** [45:09]. "Emails are for value and for reminders, okay? Texts are just for
reminders." Each email teaches a piece of what the headline promised; the reminder is one line before the sign-off
[43:19-44:25].

**The 5 x 5 ad matrix** [54:50]. Five angles (five different reasons someone would convert) times five variations
(for example five filming environments) is 25 ads. Angles must be truly different descriptions of the same thing,
not rewordings; he shows it with an iPad as the object [55:40].

**Hook, body, CTA** [59:25]. Every ad script has those three parts; AI writes the scripts [58:42].

**Three ad set structures** [1:01:09-1:02:54]. All ads in one ad set and let Meta pick (his default); one ad set
per angle; or the **Thunderdome**: one ad per ad set with a 3-second video view exclusion so every creative is
forced to spend on first impressions [1:01:31]. Ad set budgets rather than CBO [1:02:54].

**Both truths are true** [1:04:51]. Some choices have no right answer, only a trade-off: optimizing for lead /
complete registration (his primary; high volume back to the pixel, exposes weak messaging) vs schedule / purchase
(a "delayed firing event": more qualified, but too little data reaches the platform at small scale) [1:05:24-1:07:49];
dropping the price on the webinar or not [1:19:03].

**Retention rate** [1:10:58]. Of the peak live viewers, the share still there when the pitch starts. Target 80%+.

**Two halves of a webinar** [1:13:55]. The presentation (teaching, framing), about an hour; the close, about an
hour or more, and it keeps going while people stay.

**The bridge (ask to pitch)** [1:14:54]. Credited to Russell Brunson: after teaching, ask permission to make an
offer ("give me ones in the chat ... can I make you an offer?") and move into the close when the chat says yes.

**The "so you can" stack** [1:17:57]. Each offer item is introduced as: a problem, circumstance or wanted outcome
they have, then the item that answers it, "so you can" get the outcome. "You've got this going on. We're going to
give you this so you can get this." Then a slide with the whole stack.

**Price or no price** [1:19:03]. Saying the price gives fewer, more qualified calls; not saying it gives more, less
qualified calls. He drops the price because he trusts his one-to-many selling.

**The close loop** [1:20:06-1:23:02]. After the stack: objection handling, risk reversal, Q&A, repitch; then repeat
(risk reversals usually run out, so later rounds are objections, Q&A, repitch), then an outro. An objection is a
reason not to buy; a risk reversal removes the buyer's risk (a pay-per-lead agency vs a retainer agency [1:21:20]).

**Sales call as onboarding** [1:25:38]. After a webinar the call should be framed as onboarding into the offer,
where cash is collected and next steps set. "Grocery store cashiers" should close these [1:23:58].

**Sales call cleanup** [1:26:00]. Setters work the days after the webinar: first those who showed but did not book,
then those who opted in but did not show; send the replay; call and text from real iPhones [1:27:40-1:28:18].
Said to "typically double" the return on ad spend [1:29].

**Bottleneck analysis and the doubles game** [1:29:55-1:31:41]. Lay out every step's stat against its benchmark and
fix the one that looks "easiest to double" (or, for CPM, to halve).

**The scale ceiling** [1:37:08]. Scaling past a point loses money on every extra dollar; the ceiling is set by the
messaging angle and the funnel type. At the ceiling, maintain the campaign and go find new messaging or a new
funnel [1:38:04-1:38:32].

## The process, in order

**The text reminders** [45:27-45:57]
1. right after opt-in
2. the day before, about 24 hours out
3. the morning of
4. one hour before
5. ten minutes before
6. at the start
7. for no-shows, about ten minutes in: "are you having trouble joining?", with a person or an AI agent answering
   that number. The join link is never sent by text, because phone viewers convert and stay worse [48:09].

**The emails** [43:19-52:04]: about six, two a day across the promotion days, each teaching one part of the
headline's promise, plus Zoom's own reminder emails (most likely to land in the primary inbox). Someone who opts in
late only gets the messages from that point on.

**Tools he names**: pages built with Claude and Jeremy AI (connected by an MCP) and hosted on Vercel or Cloudflare
[23:15, 28:34]; Wistia for the video [24:38]; Zoom Webinars for the live webinar ("we're not tech hoppers" [47:00]);
Roezan for texts [47:16]; GoHighLevel, ActiveCampaign or HubSpot as the CRM [~48:30]; dialer.io for calls [1:28:02].

**The opt-in page** [22:54-30:14]: three colours at most; headline, date, opt-in; video (VSL) with a thumbnail that
is a curiosity hook; a countdown (never a "just in time" webinar [25:15]); the opt-in box floats beside the content
on desktop and is fixed on mobile with buttons back to it; first name, last name, email, phone, plus a TCPA
checkbox for texts; validation on email and phone; supporting text and visuals for two to three scrolls. The
headline alone should let someone opt in with confidence [25:33]. New AI-built pages convert better than the same
content in old drag-and-drop builders [28:00]; page speed matters.

**The close** [1:13:55-1:23:02]: presentation, bridge, "so you can" stack item by item, the whole-stack slide,
price (or not), then objection handling, risk reversal, Q&A, repitch in a loop, then the outro.

**After the webinar** [1:26:00-1:28:18]: calls booked during and right after; then four cleanup days before the
next webinar: dial the showed-not-booked list, then the opted-in-not-showed list; send the replay; text from real
phones.

**If it failed** [1:29:14-1:35:00]: most failed tests break even or make a little [1:29:27]; run the bottleneck
analysis; fix the stat easiest to double; relaunch ads when the opt-in rate drops from ad fatigue; go again.

**Scaling** [1:35:37-1:39:23]: daily budgets up 10-30% a day; or more webinars a week (one, two, three); automate a
scheduled webinar once it works (still never just in time); lifetime budgets let the next webinar start at any
budget; watch for the scale ceiling; use cost-per-result goals rather than plain automated bidding [1:39:17];
update the financial model as stats move.

## What is on screen (from the frames)

From all 612 frames (four agents, logs in the appendix). There is not one designed slide in 100 minutes.

**How it is shot.** About 60-65% of the video is content on screen with Jeremy in a picture-in-picture; about a
third is him on camera. Teaching stretches change the picture every 10-30 s; stories stay on camera for 1-4 minutes
(the car example 10:28-11:38, the co-living story 34:34-38:44). The content fills the frame as a rounded panel on
near-black. A rounded PiP of him sits bottom right at about a fifth of the width. It switches between a close-up
of him writing at the iPad or lectern and a wide shot of him pointing at the giant screen. When a highlight sits
bottom right, the PiP moves to bottom left so it never covers it [frames 23:56-24:26]. On camera: white tee, lav
mic, dark slatted wall, the lit ring logo "JEREMY'S INNER CIRCLE" at head height. A wall-sized LED screen at the
left always mirrors the current visual, so the content stays in frame even in talking-head shots, and he can stand
inside his own drawing and point at it [18:37, 58:11, 1:16:48].

- Almost all the teaching visual is one iPad note on a yellow legal-pad page with a red margin line, written by hand live.
