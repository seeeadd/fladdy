# Teardown: AI is (mostly) Bullshit

Jason Fladlien · uploaded 2026-10-04 · 31:35 · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v2yNV7rkyzI
Files here: `transcript.md` (timestamps), `study.md` (frames + words), `sheets/` (frames). Torn down 2026-10-29
(`sops/video-teardown.md`). Stamps are `[m:ss]` from the captions; frame stamps can sit up to about 10 s off. The
captions bleep his swearing as "[ __ ]"; quotes keep the bleep, and the pad writes it as "BS" or "Bullshit".

## In one paragraph

A 31-minute contrarian essay for business owners who use AI, or feel they should: AI is "the most overhyped
technology since sliced bread" [0:11] as a strategist, a maker and an exponential lever, and worth using only in
narrower roles. The spine is four pairs. Each opens with a spoken verdict that he writes in red ("AI strategy is
BS", macro, maker, exponential) and turns in the next breath to what to do instead, written in green: strategy to
stimulation [1:03, 3:50], macro to micro [6:02, 9:57], maker to miner [14:48, 17:15], exponential to incremental
with caveats [23:06, 26:49]. Each pair is proved with his own operation (his 47 webinar prompts [12:34], a couple
thousand reels a week [15:26], a $57 million launch [27:24]) and an outside case (the Harvard Business Review
"trendslop" article [1:10], Dr. Mike Israetel's AI-written book [6:17]). It ends on one rule: use AI as a stand-in
until a better human is found, then "plug out the AI, plug in the human" [31:19]. The hook promises not to sell an
AI course, app or agent [0:36], and he keeps it: the only pitches are a mid-roll ad for his $10,000 in-person
mastermind [14:27] and his book Use Brain, named at [28:37] and printed on the cap he wears in every frame on the
main set.

## The frameworks

**Shovels and gold** [0:00]. "AI is a great shovel to sell." When too many shovels are sold and not enough gold is
dug, "we have a problem. AI is that very problem" [0:03-0:09]. He comes back to it twice: over-engineered prompts
only pay if you sell the tool ("Is it really helpful if I'm selling a shovel to you? Sure, that's where the real
money is" [13:50-13:54]), and in the close, selling AI "is the best place to be right now, I'll be it temporarily"
[30:38-30:43].

**Trendslop** [1:10]. From a Harvard Business Review article (on screen [1:19]): researchers asked LLMs for
strategic advice and got "trend slop": "the tendency for AI to offer these generic, buzzworthy, sounding-like sound
strategy advice that parrots like popular management books like Who Moved My Cheese ... instead of giving
practical, context-specific solutions" [1:19-1:37].

**The simulation test** [1:42]. "Take the best campaigns that you know of that have been run in history, run them
as simulations in AI, and ask it for its advice and hypotheticals on which strategy it should take, and it will
always default to the safe, normal strategy" [1:43-1:54]. He hands it to the viewer: "I'll let you prove it to
yourself" [1:40].

**Context-dependent, not content-dependent** [2:05]. Given content to analyse, AI does "reasonably well, but it
breaks context" [2:20-2:25]. Strategy depends on context: in some markets the go-to was a webinar, in others no
webinar; free webinars in some, paid in others [2:29-2:45]. So "this whole stupid idea of X is dead or Y is dead
or Z is dead is dumb because nothing is dead or alive" [2:48-2:53]: a tactic is valuable or not "related to the
other pieces that are on the board in the market".

**Safe and sound vs unsound** [3:07-3:45]. Language models are "based on pattern recognition and predictability,
and strategy is not about predictability. Strategy is about doing the thing that is unpredictable, that the market
doesn't know what it wants until you put in front of it." AI defaults "to safe and sound. But strategy is,
initially at least, unsound" (everybody thinks you are "a raving madman the day before the breakthrough", then
calls you a genius [3:38]).

**Stimulation, and right-fit strategy** [3:45-4:14]. "Don't use AI for strategy. Instead, use it for stimulation."
He already knows the strategy "generally for most of my clients"; the work is to "right-fit" it: "take the thing
that is generally and directionally correct and make it specifically and exactly precise", because "the difference
between a meter and a millimeter is a little bit of word, but a lot of distance" [4:00]. The 50-ideas loop is how
(see "The process").

**Breakthroughs come by accident** [4:47-5:56]. "Almost every great strategical initiative was exposed by
accident": Viagra, penicillin, and most of the multi-million-dollar launches of his companies, which came from "an
opportunity we weren't aware of" whose signals they recognised [5:06-5:25]. AI helps with "that cross-pollination
of weird ideas that normally don't mix together to give the accidental breakthrough" [5:39]. Then the line he flags
as provocative: "A real person is still better at this" [5:48]. "AI is better than nothing" for stimulation [5:54].

**Macro vs micro** [6:07, 9:57]. AI is "[ __ ] at what we call macro content creation. It is not good at big
picture stuff" [6:07-6:14]. "If you're going to use AI to create content, have it create micro, not macro"
[14:21-14:27].

**Context rot** [7:40-8:21]. "The longer you chat with it, the worse the response" [7:42]. It confuses old ideas
with new ones, re-proposes discarded ideas, goes back to things you told it to stop "like a little petulant child",
and "loses its threat" (as captioned; the pad says "loses the thread") [7:49-8:14]. The fixes people use: new chats
often (what he does: "I'm like a schizophrenic with my AI chats" [8:37]), Claude Code, "which will show you the
context window in real time" [8:44], or routing agents.

**Space architecture** [9:02] (as the captions spell it). "Building things to circumvent things to circumvent
things to circumvent things to get an output that a human can do without having to hack it in order to get the
outcome." He uses it again at [26:47]: "your AI space architecture isn't going to get you there".

**The bucket brigade** [9:14-9:53]. Routing agents, each with a fresh context, handing the work on like a bucket
brigade at a fire; "I think catastrophic" [9:16]. "If it's losing the context, you are asking it to do too much"
[9:51]. The **Rube Goldberg machine** is the same mistake for a book, assembled "piece and agent by piece and
agent" [10:03-10:20].

**Micro in practice: his webinar prompts** [10:25-12:46]. One or two prompts per webinar section, about 47 in all,
a person checking each (steps in "The process"). His own verdict: "a lot of work for a microscopic movement forward
in the webinar" [12:00], "a slight edge over a human doing it" [13:03]. It is still worth it because "I'll take
slight edges and they make sense when you multiply them" [13:08], and because his clients, "D- minus or C- minus at
these openings", get a net win [11:43-11:55]. Only for one major problem, and only "AIs with humans in the loop,
because it's either me or somebody on my staff that is sanity checking every AI along the way" [13:32-13:47].

**AI slop** [14:04]. "Most of my clients now are worse at webinars because they're using AI slop that looks good,
but isn't good, and then they're not selling on these webinars as well as they used to" [13:58-14:10]. They now pay
him to get back.

**Maker vs miner** [14:49, 17:15]. "AI as a maker is [ __ ] It cannot make content very well to save its life"
[14:49-14:58]. "It's really good at mining content. This is where it wins" [17:15]: it can "take a lot of data and
synthesize it and crunch it for you" [17:22]. "It's supporting the content. It is not creating the content"
[21:03].

**The report card** [15:02-17:40]. He grades each maker use: email copy "about a C+ rating" [15:18]; reels: less
than 2% AI-scripted [15:31]; vibe-coded apps "about a C minus" [15:57]; website design the most useful, but only as
an MVP or "what we'd call vaporware ... mock-up style" [16:40-17:04], and "it is not maker content". The miner use
gets the top grade: summaries "at about an A- minus" [17:37]. The pad writes the grades (see "What is on screen").

**Dying in the desert** [15:59-16:35]. A C- solution "can make you millions of dollars" in a market with no
solution ("somebody goes and gives you tap water out of a hose, and you think it's the greatest thing alive"), but
it is "very fragile. It's very short-lived"; someone with discipline who builds a proper app will crush "junk food
apps".

**Noise and signal** [18:14-18:28]. His summaries switch the ratio: "I've reduced noise and I've increased signal.
Most AI is doing the opposite. It's increasing signal this much, but it's increasing noise this much. And this is
why you're becoming less productive with it."

**Hypothesise before you consume** [18:36-19:15]. "The most effective way you can learn anything is before you study
it, you create an idea around what will be covered in it, and you create your own conclusions in advance." AI
summaries make that easy; "my retention and my understanding of ideas has increased".

**AI formalises process, it never invents it** [21:40-21:54]. "The AI sucks at creating process, but the AI is
really good at pulling out existing process and turning it into a formalized document."

**Exponential vs incremental** [23:06-25:49]. AI "sucks at being exponential, which is funny because this is how
it's totally sold" [23:14]. "In general, you should never try to increment anything in your business. It's poor
strategy" [23:29-23:37], because incremental gains "get in the way of the exponential result". Six ways to grow 10%
lose to "one incredibly new way to 10x" [23:51-24:07]. You need 10x because downturns wipe out 10-40% gains:
"incremental dies" [25:16-25:31]. "AI isn't going to find your 10x move. It never will. Most humans can't find
10x" [24:56-25:09]; what AI does exponentially is "creating exponential noise, which decreases productivity"
[25:44].

**The exponential risk reversal** [24:15-24:46]. His 10x example. Instead of improving the sales copy on a funnel,
a guarantee ("better your money back guarantees, you know, 10x guarantees, uh we'll buy your business and pay you
$25,000 on top of that guarantee"); then "the funnel can still be dog [ __ ]" and you can "do millions of dollars
like I've done. Because the exponential risk reversal was a thousand times more effective than any singular
incremental move ever could have been."

**Agents** [25:49-26:03]. "You don't need 55 agents. You don't even need one agent. Agent is a dumb concept that
humans come up with to give a human characteristic to a non-human entity."

**Predicting the future is expensive** [26:14-26:47]. No "100-hour weeks of investing in speculative AI
technology". "We want to explore different innovations in a low-risk, low-cost fashion, make hundreds of bets on
speculative ideas that don't hurt us, that we can find the answer to quickly, and if only one of those pay off, we
make a thousand times more than what the market typically produces."

**Incremental, with caveats** [26:49-30:29]. He says "we are allergic to incremental ... but there is one exception
here" [24:50-24:56], and later that what will help "is incremental" [26:49-26:54]. Where: "an email when none
exists" [27:14], where "good enough is sufficient and C+ wins" [27:47], and only once "we've exhausted our ability
to ship quality" and "have a money printer" [27:54-28:06]. The caveats: narrow scope [29:57], resist the urge to let
it write for you ("a siren song" [29:28]), and know that "this incremental advantage can vaporize in a second",
because "if everybody can increase productivity by 2% relatively easily, then that is a net zero gain cuz you
don't do business in a vacuum" [30:12-30:29]. He expects most viewers to break the caveats, so "it's better to just
use it to write the kids PE teacher, you know, excuse my son for being late note as opposed to having it try to
perform surgery on your business" [27:01-27:12].

**Hammer, not screwdriver** [30:59-31:10]. "If we right-size it, we recognize it that that it's a hammer, it's not
a screwdriver, it's a hammer and it can be used to nail in certain nails, and this is a tool in our toolkit."

**Plug out the AI, plug in the human** [31:12-31:32]. Mostly AI is a placeholder "until we find a human that can do
it better. And then we plug out the AI, plug in the human. Because in almost every scenario, it is cheaper and more
effective at the end of the day with the proper calculus to have the right human do it for you as opposed to have
the right AI."

## The process, in order

**The 50-ideas loop: right-fitting a strategy** [4:07-4:46]
1. Start from the strategy you already know is "generally and directionally correct" [4:07].
2. Ask: "Here's my idea. Give me 50 different examples of how this idea can show up." [4:19]
3. Expect 45 terrible, 4 promising, 1 really promising [4:26-4:33].
4. Ask it to refactor those and give "50-ish more" [4:33-4:38].
5. Repeat until something "is interesting to me to pursue from a strategic standpoint" [4:38-4:46].

**When a chat rots: the options, in his order** [8:14-9:53]
1. Start new chats often (what he does) [8:24-8:30].
2. Use Claude Code to watch the context window fill and learn what is context-heavy [8:44-9:00].
3. Route agents with fresh context, the bucket brigade, which he calls catastrophic [9:14-9:38].
4. His answer: ask for less. "If it's losing the context, you are asking it to do too much" [9:51]. Go micro.

**His micro webinar build** [10:25-13:47]
1. Pop quiz, the opening (2-3 minutes of run time): one prompt of about 2,000 words for "a decent enough version
   one" [10:32-10:48].
2. Challenges you face (10-15 challenges): a buyer profile prompt run before the webinar is created, the AI
   analyses the market, then a second prompt writes the challenges; about 6,000 words for the two [10:51-11:14].
3. Excitement stack: "By the end of this webinar, you're going to discover this, this, and this and you'll be able
   to do this, this, and this and this and this and this will no longer be a problem" [12:07-12:16].
4. Why listen to me: "here's how I discovered this, here's where I was at, here's where I'm at now, here's why I'm
   uniquely qualified to help you, here's why this specific thing is the thing I'm teaching you over ... other
   thing" [12:16-12:31].
5. "And then we go on": about 47 connected prompts [12:31-12:46].
6. A person on his staff sanity-checks every AI step [13:40-13:47].
The folder on screen shows the sections past step 4, not said: see "What is on screen" [10:34].

**Research with summaries** [17:29-19:13]
1. Have AI ("Notebook LLM") analyse about 50 videos, or podcasts, on a topic [18:01, 18:31].
2. Read the summaries and pull out the key ideas "ahead of consuming any one of those videos" [18:04-18:10].
3. Watch only a couple [18:11].
4. Before watching, write your bets: "I bet this will cover this. I bet this will be the insight. I bet this will
   be the aha moment. I bet it will advise me against this." [18:50-18:56]
5. While watching, check if you were right; afterwards, compare the hypothesis with what happened [18:59-19:06].

**The car-video clip audit** [19:30-20:49]
1. His wife drives and asks a question; two DJIs on suction cups on the window; he answers "hot take fashion", a
   couple of times a week [19:38-19:52].
2. The transcripts go through an AI system "programmed to tell us, 'You should get between 40 and 45 clips from
   this session'" [20:22-20:31].
3. The editors are held to that count: when they deliver 20, "Bro, you're missing half of the clips. Go back there
   and do the rest, otherwise I'm not going to assign you any more content." [20:34-20:44]

**First-draft SOPs** [21:08-21:54]
1. Walk through a process on a training call with a client or a staff member [21:13-21:18].
2. Gemini summarises it ("a Gemini function essentially when we upload it in Google Drive") into a first-draft SOP
   [21:20-21:26].
3. A person finalises it [21:28].
The process has to exist first: AI formalises it, it never creates it [21:40-21:54].

**Book research** [21:57-22:50]: Google's AI summaries for rapid-fire questions [22:10-22:16]; ChatGPT for "a chart
that will summarize this concept" [22:17-22:22]; a technical explanation for "that little like call out box that
you would have in a book" [22:24-22:29]; each chapter's manuscript fed in for its key points [22:31-22:38]; his own
step-by-step turned into a technical explanation [22:40-22:48].

**The incremental caveats** [26:49-30:29]
1. Use it where nothing exists yet ("an email when none exists") [27:14].
2. Only once you are out of capacity to ship quality and "have a money printer" [27:54-28:06].
3. Keep it in narrow scope [29:57].
4. Resist the urge to let it write for you; each "no" costs cognition until the habit forms [29:00-29:49].
5. Expect the edge to vanish once everyone has it [30:12-30:29].

**The opening, beat by beat** [0:00-1:19]
1. A confession that makes him the unlikely critic: "AI is a great shovel to sell. Given my talents, I know how to
   sell it better than any other." [0:00]
2. The problem as a picture: too many shovels, not enough gold [0:03-0:09].
3. A superlative and a betrayal the viewer already knows: "the most overhyped technology since sliced bread", worse

4. The pain: "AI is promising you productivity out the ass while making you ... less productive" [0:21-0:25].
5. The turn: "But my friend, all is not lost" (overlay), with gold to be dug "if you can switch your paradigm"
   [0:25-0:33].
6. The anti-pitch: "without selling you an AI course, an app, or an agent at the end of this video" (overlay list)
   [0:36-0:39].
7. Credentials in two numbers: AI companies pay him $5K an hour; a client went from $1M to $2M a month "with just one
   shift in their AI approach" [0:39-0:55].
8. The bridge into part 1: "as good as AI is, it can't replace strategy. Which is where we're going to start."
   [0:55-1:00]
9. The first verdict spoken in fragments ("AI strategy" / "is" / "[ __ ]" [1:03-1:08]) as he writes it in red, then
   the HBR article on screen as proof [frames 1:09, 1:19].

**What keeps it moving**
- Every part opens on the same spoken verdict: "AI strategy is [ __ ]" [1:03], "it is [ __ ] at what we call macro

  [23:06-23:12], and the close "So, AI is mostly [ __ ]" [30:31].

- Provocation announced before it lands: "I'm going to say something that's going to piss some people off" [5:45].
- A guess asked of the viewer before the number: "Guess how many of them are scripted with AI? Less than 2%" [15:30].
- A signpost for the last part: "And last, exponential." [23:06]
- Self-mockery about his own system: "Like this is insane" [11:27]; a laugh after "a slight edge over a human doing
  it" [13:03-13:07].
- One plain analogy per point: hair dryer in the shower [7:03], bucket brigade [9:24], Rube Goldberg [10:05],
  dying in the desert [16:07], ten rabbits [23:44], the PE teacher's note vs surgery [27:04], junk food at 9 pm

- It ends on a one-word question, "Thoughts?" [31:34]: no subscribe ask, no link, no end card in the frames.

- Share of 191 frames: talking head 141 (about 74%; 5 of them carry an overlay or the inset window), handwritten pad 44 (about 23%; 1 with an overlay), screen captures or graphics 4 (about 2%), the ad insert 2 (about 1%). No designed slides, no picture-in-picture, no lower thirds, no running captions, no end card in the frames.
- Cadence: in teaching stretches the picture changes every 10-20 s, cutting between camera and pad between bullets; the pad never holds more than about 30 s. Stories stay on camera: about 3.5 minutes (17:31-20:50), about 1:40 (12:43-14:13), several of 60-80 s.
- The set never changes on the main shots: seated at a round white table, bookshelf behind, warm light from the right, the red "Use Brain" cap. The framing alternates between left, tight centred and right at cuts.

- Overlays: a bold rounded sans in white and red (or black with a red box), used only at the turn (0:29), the anti-pitch (0:39), the 47-prompt total (12:34) and the ad price (14:33). Proof screens carry a yellow highlighter on the claim's words (1:19, 6:36); his own prompt folder carries yellow row highlights that advance as he names sections (10:34-12:14).
