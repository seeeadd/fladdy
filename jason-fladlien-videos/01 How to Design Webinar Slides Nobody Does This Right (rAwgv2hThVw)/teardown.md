## In one paragraph

Jason teaches "persuasive design" for webinar slides by redesigning slides that nine of his followers sent in, before
and after [0:35]. His case is that designers "go for form and not function" and that a pretty deck can "crush your
conversions" [0:22]. The spine is a handful of rules he repeats on every deck: the most important words are the
biggest, and titles, names, numbers and system names usually are not [1:55]; "show not tell" with real screenshots,
real photos, emojis and tables in place of metaphors, stock and AI pictures [2:08, 20:05]; one crowded slide becomes
many, revealed one item at a time ("Slides are free" [6:09]; about 500 slides a webinar, motion every six seconds
[28:44]); two colours and one font; and the mechanism (framework) slide that comes back with progress [23:04]. It ends
on his call to action slide template [41:47]. It is shot as a talking head plus his Keynote slides on an iPad, marked
up live in red ink beside a webcam picture-in-picture; about 80% of the frames are slides.

## The frameworks

**Form vs function, "persuasive design"** [0:17-0:35]. "You can't hire this out to regular designers because they go
for form and not function. See, you'll get something that looks really nice, but will also crush your conversions in
a bad way." He calls the redesigns "Flatline best practices" (as captioned) [0:48] and "the flatline redesign" [23:45].
The close restates it: "these are design principles that are functional. They're made to persuade" [42:18].

**Top to bottom, never left to right** [1:26]. "We should not design things to go from left to right when it comes to
list and bullet points and such. We should design them from go from top to bottom." Repeated for Alex ("They read top
down" [25:39]) and Shelby's ladder, which reads bottom up [32:12]. The one exception: side by side is fine "because
it's a sequence of events" [32:52].

**The least important thing is the biggest (the mistake he hammers)** [1:42-1:59]. "They make the least important
thing the biggest thing and therefore the center and focus of attention when it should be the other way around." The
culprits he names: the title ("Usually the title is the least important thing" [13:18]; "You've been taught wrong
about PowerPoint your whole life" [13:09]); the presenter's name ("they already know who he is" [11:31]); the system's
name [23:42]; numbers ("numbers are the least important part" [27:40]; "Why we like to make numbers the biggest thing
on our slide? I will never understand" [35:13]); the step numbers [33:24-33:32]; and the price [41:22].

**Show not tell** [2:08]. "A principle I'm going to teach you right away that will completely shift all of your
design if you get it." Instead of writing what they will discover, show it. Later in his words: "I didn't say you
post. I showed the post from LinkedIn" [17:35]; "We want to show it in the real world visually as much as possible.
Not metaphorically, not conceptually, not with AI generated images. Screenshots are your best friend on a webinar"
[20:05-20:17].

**Slide inside a slide (the intro tease)** [2:48-3:28]. He found the slides that teach each promised point,
screenshotted them, and put each on a slide under a small title. The words go either under the slide or in the
presenter notes [3:08-3:21]. He "intentionally picked slides that were very visual at a glance that told a story and
made the audience say, 'Oo, I want to know more about that'" [3:24].

**Three exposures** [3:37-3:58]. "The first time somebody sees it, they're likely to forget it." Show the slide as a
setup in the intro, again in the teaching, and a third time in the recap ("so far today we've discussed"). "Seeing it
in three different ways from three different angles and three different perspectives massively increases the buyin."

**Open loops by design** [4:07-4:38]. One slide that shows "all of the slides that I could fit on the screen", some
"cut off intentionally at the very bottom. So you only see half of them." "We use words too much and we use visuals
too little. We should use the visuals that are the most functional" [4:19].

**Minimalism: remove what does not persuade** [5:34-5:52]. Elements that "don't add to the persuasive capabilities
and so therefore they detract." Minimalism is function ("it doesn't distract you from the messaging") and practice
("The design becomes a lot quicker"). What he removes across the video: decorative swooshes and logos [5:37], stock
photos [5:56], taglines [11:20], the presenter's name [11:31], highlighter graphics and icons [13:22, 22:45], glows
[23:31], background images [22:37], level labels [24:08], step numbers [28:10, 34:17], the presenter's photo on the CTA
[41:10], extra calls to action [41:34].

**Slides are free** [6:05-6:51]. "I don't use just one slide to tell a story. Slides are free. So, we should use as
many of them as we possibly can." Marvin's one bio slide becomes eight. "I'd prefer you have more slides instead of
less slides" [29:50].

**Two colours** [7:06]. "This is a great way to keep design simple but make the important information that you want to
convey stand out." The most important piece "gets bolded that's a little bit bigger in font and it's a different
color" [10:44].

**Information design inside one sentence** [7:55-8:06]. The less important half of a line goes smaller, same font,
maybe a lighter face: "Even though it's one sentence, I'm making it as easy as possible for the audience to take in."

**Real proof over pictures (and where AI is allowed)** [7:22-9:07]. "I do not like AI for design hardly at all. But
there's a couple instances where it's okay to work" [7:22]: changing Marvin's expression when the real photos from
2000 and 1993 were not available [7:29-7:46], and animating his real credit report [10:07]. The ideal is the real
thing: him in the seminar rooms, "the amounts of money that he paid because this will become a price anchor later"
[8:16-8:29], the receipts [8:37], the actual houses before and after the flip [8:55]. "We only go to stock when we
really don't have anything else" [9:07].

**Functional animation** [9:25-9:29]. "I'm a huge fan of functional animation." Hidden first, then it pops up. "The
beauty of animation isn't because it looks cool. It's because it can focus the attention on the information
sequentially" [21:18].

**Make numbers visible** [9:39-10:05]. "People don't understand numbers very well ... if I tell you think of 30, you
have trouble thinking of it." Show 30 houses, or a calendar ("I lost a house a day for 30 days"). Later: 350 clocks
for 350 build hours [35:42], a grid of 100 people [18:42], two, five and ten people for roles [37:54].

**Functional beats cool** [12:45]. "This looks cool but it's not functional. This looks less cool but it is way more
functional and it's also easier to design."

**Bigger fonts** [14:02-14:33]. "Bigger fonts are better than smaller fonts functionally. Design-wise, they don't look
as nice." The best buyers are "30, 40, 50 plus" with more money and worse eyesight, "including me".

**Two speeds of redesign** [13:43-15:22]. "Under the gun" (a deadline): small title, regular bullets, bold lead-ins,
no italics. "More advanced": one bullet per slide, the finished ones stacked small at the top so "you can know which
one you are on"; "more design work, but tends to have more of an impact" [15:17-15:22].

**Ugly can sell better than pretty** [15:46-16:12]. "If you look too professional on the designs, you're less
trustworthy on the webinar ... you are not selling design to designers." "It's better to look ugly with design than it
is to look pretty because they'll get distracted by the glitz and the glamour and they'll also trust you less."

**Metaphors must match, or go** [16:20-16:52, 24:08, 32:03, 40:22]. "If we're going to use the metaphor of the
hamster wheel, then visually we have to stay consistent with it." He dropped it and showed the real thing. "He's using
the word levels, but he's not showing it in levels" [24:19]. A ladder "is visually clunky" and reads bottom up [32:12];
"I don't think we need a metaphor at all" [32:08]. A wheel: "people typically don't learn information like this"
[40:26].

**Storyboarding: extend the visuals** [18:42-18:54, 21:54]. Reuse the same pictures across slides so the slides tell
one story (the post becomes RIP, then seven RIPs, then years of calendars and 100 people). "You can do so much with
arrows and screenshots that it's insane" [18:54].

**Screenshot the reality** [19:05-20:50]. "Show it in the reality in which it's contained": do the real YouTube
search, screenshot the pieces, make "an amalgamation of a couple different screenshots", "remove as much noise as
possible" [19:36-19:43]. For a statistic, "I go to the actual source and I screenshot it. I still put the source
underneath of it. I highlight the key part" [20:43-20:50].

**Titles tell the story** [22:52, 26:05-26:16]. "You want to tell a story with the titles as much as possible." Alex's
whole slide becomes one question, "If you know what to do, why doesn't it stick?": "This progresses the story in an
active voice."

**Mechanism slides** [23:04-25:05]. "From a content perspective, these are the most important slides that you can
have and I spend a lot of time designing on them because I'm going to be using them over and over again." The name
goes very small and off-centre [23:45]; arrows small [23:59]; no "level one, level two"; a tagline under each step.
The design must be "flexible and easy to adjust" [24:31] because of what happens live: "I'll check it, make it smaller,
show the second point bigger that we're covering now. And then the third point is tease. I made it a little opaque so
you see it, but you can't fully see it ... Notice I also use the color of yellow here on the arrow. So now they always
know where they're at. They always know where they're going" [24:35-25:00]. A framework slide is revisited "over and
over again" [27:28].

**Proof is the only allowed noise** [27:47-28:06]. "If I'm going to use a lot of noise on a slide, which I tend to
avoid, it's going to be overwhelming proof." No numbering when "you can count three" [28:10]; one word per step
("nutrition, strength, identity").

**500 slides, motion every six seconds** [28:44-28:52]. "My average webinar presentation is about 500 slides and there
are significant animations of showing information one after the other on a slide. Typically you see motion on my
designs every six seconds."

**One slide when splitting breaks the context** [28:57-29:28]. "This is one of the rare instances where I don't think
you need two slides because the context gets broken across these two." "Two words, two dollar figures, one slide."

**Tables** [29:55-30:03]. "Tables are great at communicating information, especially when you animate them."

**The dramatic reveal** [31:00-31:48]. "This will work so good in so many of your designs." Duplicate the slide, put a
"100% solid white" box over what should fade, opacity "down to about 80%", and "lay new information on top of old
information. And this is really like a dramatic reveal."

**One font, a few faces** [33:10-33:16]. "Just one font. Couple different faces. So there's bold, there's semibold,
and then there's normal. And then that's it." Colours separate the steps of a process [33:37-33:40]. Italics: "I tend
to not use them" [13:35].

**Elaborate designs trap you** [33:47-34:10]. "If you have a very elaborate design, you have to stay within the
containment of that design." Shelby's mini ladder "takes on the slide ... space ... and it doesn't contain any
information." His fix reuses "the same element we had on that key mechanism slide" [34:25].

**The cost of pretty** [36:47-37:06]. "I do like to make things look nice, like Apple kind of style," but "making it
look pretty like this takes like 10 more minutes or 30 more minutes per slide. This would have worked just as well if
we would have just made it a simple bullet point list."

**"How do we show this?"** [37:09]. "This is always the question I ask myself when I design slides." For "12 weekly
live build sessions every single Wednesday": the weeks, the days, the sessions popping in, then the calendar
"getting filled with more more more value" [37:09-37:31].

**AI slides can't be edited** [37:39]. "The other problem with AI slides is you can't really edit them." Against that,
on Alex's deck: "Claude can do a really good design because Claude really focuses on the information as a component
of its design" [25:24]; and Andrew's "was so AI it was driving me insane" [15:28].

**Emojis as type** [37:54-38:24]. "You can copy and paste them and then you can change the font size of an emoji. So
this is like a font ... I use a lot of emojis these days for design, not because they're cute, but because they're
functional."

**The rule of three** [38:45-38:53]. Emma compared two numbers; he added a third. "For some reason people pick up on
three. I think two is a coincidence, but three is a pattern."

**A nice background, done right** [39:22-40:08]. Behind the words, "you don't want a key part of that background image
to be in the way of the words themselves." Put a black box on the image and bring its opacity down "so you the image
peaks through from it but then the font can live on top of it." "I would build a lot of slides this way. If I really
want to have that nice aesthetic." Then: "When people are on webinars, they care about information. They don't care
about design" [40:15].

**No abbreviations they may not know** [40:45]. "I would not make it sig speech because they don't know what sig
necessarily is."

**The call to action slide** [40:58-42:14]. "My favorite slide ... Everybody should have it." The template is in "The
process, in order" below.

## The process, in order

**Tease the deck in the intro** [2:48-4:38]
1. Find the slides that teach each thing they will discover, and screenshot them.
2. Put each on its own slide under a small title ("What you'll discover today").
3. Say the words, written under the picture or kept in the presenter notes.
4. Pick slides "very visual at a glance that told a story".
5. End with one slide of every slide that fits, the last row cut off at the bottom.

**Show a slide three times** [3:37-3:58]: as a setup in the intro, in the teaching, and in the recap ("so far today
we've discussed").

**Break a crowded bullet slide** [13:43-15:22]
- Under a deadline: small title, regular bullets, bold lead-in words, no italics.
- Advanced: one point per slide; the topic small top left; each finished point stacks under it, ticked; the current
  point large in the centre.

**Run a mechanism slide live** [24:35-25:00]
1. The first point is the big one.
2. When it is done, tick it and make it smaller.
3. The next point becomes the biggest; the one after it is faded ("a little opaque") as a tease.
4. A coloured (yellow) arrow points to where we are. Repeat until the last point.

**The dramatic reveal** [31:00-31:48]
1. Duplicate the slide.
2. Remove what you no longer need (he removed the arrow).
3. Put a 100% solid white box over the information that should fade.
4. Bring its opacity down to about 80%.
5. Lay the new information on top.

**A background photo under type** [39:47-40:01]
1. Place the image; keep its key parts away from where the words go.
2. Put a black box over it and lower the opacity until the image "peaks through".
3. Set the type on top, the key words in one other colour.

**Screenshot from the real world** [19:31-20:50]: do the real search or open the real source; screenshot the pieces;
remove the noise (sidebars, chrome); combine them on one slide; keep the source line under it; highlight the key line.

**Emojis as type** [37:54-38:24]: copy, paste, set the size like a letter (24, 36, 48, 72 points), reuse across slides.

**The builds he narrates** (the order things appear, as he says it)
- Credit: "my credit", 820 shows, the 8 crosses out, the 5 shows, "trashed", then "serious about credit repair"
  [10:15-10:25].
- LinkedIn, slide 1: "See, LinkedIn is a social media platform", then "you post and hope the algorithm likes you" with
  its screenshots, then "your content dies in 6 hours" (RIP) [17:01-17:29]. Slide 2: Monday RIP, then each next day,
  then the rest "bang bang bang bang bang" [17:51-18:01].
- Six years: "a place where your content doesn't die in 6 hours", then "potentially works for 6 years", then the
  calendars, then the hundred people [18:21-18:42].
- A hundred: the source with 2.7 billion first, then "you only need about a hundred", then a video with a hundred
  views, then "transform your entire practice" [20:50-21:29].
- Excuses: the title, excuse one, its answer, excuse two, its answer, through four pairs [26:21-27:02].
- The table: "Take your top five selling items", the items, 50 orders a day, +$1 per order, the question "How much
  additional revenue would this make you?", the arrow and $250, the week ($1,750), the year ($91,250); next slide the
  reveal [30:03-31:07].
- 350: "what 350 build hours buys you", all the clocks at once, "in 12 weeks", then each line [35:38-35:59].
- The calendar: weeks, days, the 12 sessions popping in; then other days filling (new content Mondays, Q&As Fridays)
  [37:09-37:31].
- Bullet slides: "bullet point one shows up as an animation. Bullet point two and then bullet point three" [12:14];
  if the list must stay on screen, "animate the highlight behind it" instead [12:10].

**The call to action slide, as he lays it out** [41:47-42:14]: "you see all of what you get over here. You see the
value stack. You see the total. The total is big. This font is bigger than the investment font. This is the most
