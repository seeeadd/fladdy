# Teardown: The New Webinar

Jason Fladlien · uploaded 2026-10-25 · 17:18 · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VtXp0UwwK3Y
Files here: `transcript.md` (timestamps), `study.md` (frames + words), `sheets/` (frames). Torn down 2026-10-29
(`sops/video-teardown.md`). Stamps are `[m:ss]` from the captions (`transcript.json`); frame stamps can sit up to
about 10 s off. Text read off his printed sheets is small at 320x180 and marked as such.

## In one paragraph

He teaches the "YouTube webinar": a long-form, usually unlisted YouTube video that does a webinar's selling job
with no registration, no Zoom and no hard pitch [0:30, 1:32, 3:44]. He starts from his traditional webinar
structure (four parts, fifteen elements, on a printed sheet) [0:49-1:13] and changes it one element at a time for
YouTube: the hook becomes the previous video, the pop quiz becomes a modified two agendas, positioning the person
becomes positioning the vehicle, the hard pitch at the end becomes a soft sell throughout with several calls to
action, the product is demonstrated instead of revealed, the offer stack leaves the video, and scarcity is built
into the offer itself (his "Richard Mille style of offer") [2:34-12:21]. Then he lays out the funnel: one core
video of one to three hours, fed by many on-ramp videos that each end by pointing to it, which he mines from his
webinar slides, one slide per 10-minute video [12:22-15:05]. His experience is that a majority buys after about
three videos [16:19]. It is for anyone who sells with webinars, told to camera, with three printed sheets marked
by hand as the only visuals.

## The frameworks

**The YouTube webinar** [0:30-1:41]. "This is a webinar that doesn't look like a webinar. No one registers for it,
and it doesn't run through a webinar platform like Zoom. And it also drives sales without appearing to be a sales
pitch" [0:30]. In his words, "a long-form presentation that is designed to convert audiences, but it's not done in
the traditional format" [1:32]. His reason: a traditional webinar asks people to register for a future event, and
they then "put time out of their schedule to be there present with you" [1:41-1:56]; someone scrolling YouTube is
"not registering for anything. They're looking for information ... but they don't want to be sold" [1:57]. Hard
selling, pitching, closing and bonus stacking make sense "in private" but not "in public", and it is not "how the
algorithm is designed to reward communication" [2:06-2:27]. The job: "take the best of the webinar formula and port
it into the YouTube environment" by knowing "the differences of media" [2:27-2:34].

**The traditional webinar structure** [0:49-1:21]. One of his five structures, "the one that covers the most ground"
[0:54]. Four parts (the captions say "central content, transition, and offer" [0:59]; the sheet's column heads are
Intro, Content, Transition, Offer) and fifteen elements, said in one breath: "hook, pain, game [gain on the sheet],
position, paradigm, mechanisms, demonstration, recap, tie-down, choices, core, price, bonus, guarantee, scarcity"
[1:05]. "You follow this formula regular webinar, it's hard not to do very well with it" [1:13]. He says he will
compare it "as a function of time" [1:02], but no times are said; the sheet carries a time range under each part
(see "Every number"). The sheet's one-line definitions (small print, best reading at 320x180, frames 0:59-1:09):

**The pop quiz (live only)** [3:53-4:22]. "One of my favorite webinar introductions": take "the five biggest issues
that your audience has that stop them from moving forward", turn them into true/false questions "to show them that
these are no longer issues by the end of the webinar", and have them answer in the chat. "There's no better one."
He dates it to 2008 ("Keanu Reeves, pop quiz, hot shot") and says it "still works in 2026" [4:17]. On YouTube it is
"a social faux pas": "You're going to look like an idiot if you say, 'Listen, I got a question for you ... write it
down in the YouTube comments'" [4:25-4:43].

**The two agendas opener, and its YouTube version** [4:46-5:55]. The original: "I have two agendas here today. My
first is to give you the best lesson ever on insert topic here. My second agenda is to sell you something. However,
if I don't make good on my first promise ... then please don't buy from me ever. However, on the off chance that I
do make good ... then you should at least consider what I'm going to offer you at the end of this webinar" [4:56].
(He introduces it as an opener [4:50]; the captions then call it "the two agendas close" [5:17].) The YouTube
version: "I'm going to break it down for you exactly how we're successful with this thing and how we're helping so
many other people get these amazing results and I'm going to show you how we can work together in different ways
throughout the video" [5:21]. The viewer reads it as "a commercial appeal", but "done through the framework of
here's how we're getting results for clients" [5:34-5:45]. "It's still an open loop, but we are more up front that
there's a way that we can work together" [5:48].

**Position the vehicle, not yourself** [6:04-6:51]. On YouTube "you have already positioned yourself as somebody
who can communicate value" by the time they land [6:28], so the positioning becomes "not you, look at me, look at
what I've done, it's the vehicle. Look at this thing that we use. ... This is why this is better than any other
thing that you can consider out there if you're looking to solve this problem" [6:35-6:51].

**Soft sell throughout, three kinds of call to action** [6:51-8:47]. The live webinar sells hard because it is
private and people "know that if I come here, he's going to show me an incredible offer" [7:06]; on YouTube "you're
mixed in with every other kind of video under the sun", so "instead of hard pitch at the end, it's soft sell
throughout the process" [7:14-7:22]. Three kinds of call to action [7:24]:
1. **Link in the description**, to "an offer doc", "a landing page" or "a funnel" [7:29];
2. **Pinned comment**, with "a different mechanism to get them to respond favorably" or the same one [7:37];
3. **DM**: "If you want to know more about this, DM me over on different social media platform" [7:49].
Plus a phone number to text, or WhatsApp, "depending on how granular and personal that you want to get" [8:03].
"We want to use more than one call to action and more than one way to do the call to action" [7:54]. His printed
diagram's "HOW IT SELLS" column names three: "Pinned Comment", "Link in Description", "DM Call to Action" (frames
14:49-16:08).

**The 1% that spends 40%** [8:13-8:47]. "For these YouTube videos, especially algorithmically how they work, you can
get that 1% of the market that spends 40% of its money", which makes high-touch contact (DM, text, a funnel to you
or a sales team) worth it, "especially if you're a beginner" [8:15-8:36]. "It's through repetition more that you're
going to get people to move forward, but you get the highest quality of leads in the market" [8:38].

**Demonstrate the product, with the client / not-client line** [9:08-9:58]. "If you think of all your deliverables of
whatever you're selling, you should be showing them in action in the YouTube video" [9:15]. The line he gives:
"This is the resource that we created that helps our clients do this thing. If you're one of our clients, this is
how we apply it for you. If you aren't our client, this is how you can create your own version of this resource"
[9:24]. It is "highly educational and it's highly detailed", but about the resources you develop, which viewers
"can either recreate themselves, find alternatives for, or give you money to get yours" [9:36-9:48].

**The stack lives off the video** [9:58-10:27]. "They see the offer stack when they move forward from the YouTube
webinar": through the link in the description, "a document", or "a different piece of collateral for marketing"
that reveals "all of the elements involved in one location" [10:02-10:18]. "It's pretty low-key" [10:24].

**The Richard Mille style of offer** [10:50-11:46]. Scarcity on a static video has to be built into the offer: "We
only have this many, we only release it at this rate, and if you get it, you're in. If not, you'll have to wait till
the next round" [10:58-11:07]; "some sort of function that you can reliably fulfill on that is inherently scarce.
You design it to be scarce by nature" [11:08]. The best version, "what we do a lot with clients": an offer only
found through "I saw the video, then I went to a YouTube webinar, and then from that, I qualify for an offer that
nobody else in public has", with "an extreme limit on the availability" [11:24-11:42]. "And then, you obviously
stick to that" [11:42].

**The flywheel** [11:46-12:21]. A fixed supply gives a reliable number: 25 packages a month at $2,000 is $50,000 a
month, which "if nothing else ... gives you a budget of putting $50,000 into your YouTube content" [11:46-12:03].
More reach brings more viewers; "If you increase demand but keep supply the same, then you can double your price
and still get the same amount of conversions. So, 50,000 a month becomes 100, becomes 200" [12:15].

**The core video** [12:22-13:11]. "Unlisted video, it can be as long as you want it to be. Better to be longer than
shorter. So, this can be an hour, it can be 2 hours long. It can be 3 hours long", because there are "multiple calls
to action throughout" [12:31-12:45]. It explains "as fully as possible everything involved and how you work with
clients ... what the resources are you developed, how those resources are useful if the user has to develop them on
their own versus how they can get access if they were to work with you" [12:45-13:00]. "Be as thorough, be as
detailed as you wish on it, cuz you can always change it, edit it down later. But, it's better to show more than to
show less" [13:00]. On the diagram it is the black box "THE CORE VIDEO" in the column "ONE CORE VIDEO".

**The on-ramps (feeder videos)** [13:09-13:59]. "Every video that you create is designed to at the end of it point
them to this video" [13:13], with a closing line such as "And if you need more help in this area, then check out
this video" or "Okay, if you want to see how we do this for clients, check out this video" [13:25-13:32]. "Every
video here is a feeder video to this offer" [13:32]. Then "you work backwards": "what are all the different mindsets
that I can put somebody in that's going to be most conducive to them consuming this video?", and what content is
"useful to stand-alone in and of itself, but also help move it forward" [13:39-13:57]. On the diagram: "MANY VIDEOS",
five boxes "VIDEO 1" to "VIDEO 5".

**One slide, one video** [14:01-14:51]. "I look at all the slides that I designed for my webinars, and then I say,
'God, if this one slide could be unpacked for 10 minutes, what would I say?'" [14:01]. A webinar cannot hold it all
("It'd be 50-hour long webinar" [14:14]), but "each slide or each key point" can be "its own standalone video"
[14:18]. Each slide can be taught from several perspectives: "the 50,000-ft view to set it up" or "granular in the
weeds" [14:33-14:38], so the ideas are "never ending" [14:41].

**Feeders of feeders** [14:54-15:05]. "There are feeder videos that you can feed into these videos that then feed
into this video right here." He writes "Feeder" twice above "VIDEO 1" on the diagram as he says it (frames
14:49-15:58).

## The process, in order

**Turning a webinar into a YouTube webinar** (his order, which is the order of the traditional structure)
[2:34-12:21]
1. Hook: drop it. The previous videos bring the viewer; packaging does not matter; publish the core video unlisted
   [3:27-3:51].
2. Introduction: no pop quiz or chat questions; open with the YouTube two agendas (how we get results, how we can
   work together throughout) [4:43-5:55].
3. Positioning: skip the 5-10 minutes on yourself; position the vehicle as better than anything else for this
   problem [6:15-6:51].
4. Selling: soft, throughout, with several calls to action of more than one kind [7:14-8:11].
5. Product: show every deliverable in action, with the client / not-client line [9:13-9:36].
6. Offer stack: none on the video; put it on the page or document behind the link [10:00-10:25].
7. Scarcity: build it into the offer (fixed supply, a release rate, reachable only through this path), and keep to
   it [10:58-11:44].

**Building the funnel** [12:22-15:05]
1. Make the core video: unlisted, one to three hours, everything about how you work and what you developed,
   calls to action throughout [12:31-13:09].
2. Put the calls to action on it: link in the description, pinned comment, DM (and text or WhatsApp if you want it
   personal) [7:24-8:11; diagram frames 14:49-16:08].
3. End every other video with a call to action to the core video [13:11-13:36].
4. Work backwards from the core video: which mindsets make someone ready to watch it, and what content is useful on
   its own but moves them toward it [13:39-13:59].
5. Mine the ideas: each webinar slide or key point becomes a standalone 10-minute video, taught from more than one
   perspective [14:01-14:51].
6. Add feeder videos for the feeder videos [14:54-15:05].
7. Grow reach, keep supply fixed, raise the price [12:07-12:21].

**The viewer's path** [16:05-16:35]
1. Watches one feeder video, goes to the core video, "they probably won't buy".
2. Watches the next video, goes to the core video, "then they might buy".
3. "It typically takes three different videos to set up the main video before a majority of people purchase."

**Researching it on other channels** [15:15-15:45]
1. "What's the video they link at the end of the current video?"
2. "Does that video go to a listed video or an unlisted video?"
3. "How did the previous video set it up?"
4. "How do they sell from that video? Do they have pin comments? Do they have link in description? Do they have DMs
   to calls to action? Do they have phone numbers ...? Do they tell you resources that you can go to and opt-in for
   and then follow up with a sales process."
